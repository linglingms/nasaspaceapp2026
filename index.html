From evidence to action

What should we plant next season?

Farmers face increasingly complex decisions as temperatures shift, rainfall becomes less predictable, and soil health declines across many regions. Water shortages, erosion, nutrient depletion, and extreme weather events add further uncertainty. Selecting new crops to add to a rotation can be difficult, because a crop that improves soil health in one place may consume too much water, introduce disease, conflict with local markets, or fall outside expected growing seasons somewhere else.

This challenge is inspired in part by multigenerational farming experience: many families have lived through seasons where better tools for tracking changing conditions could have helped identify healthier, more resilient rotation choices. Today, NASA’s long-term environmental datasets offer new ways to understand local growing conditions, and science-informed techniques open opportunities to translate that data into practical, farmer-friendly insights.

TerraCycle AI combines causal analysis and predictive analysis to help compare crop rotation strategies for healthier, more resilient fields.

Why this project exists

Helping farmers make resilient rotation decisions.

Farmers worldwide are navigating shifting temperatures, less predictable rainfall, water shortages, extreme weather, and declining soil health. TerraCycle AI turns NASA Earth observations, local soil information, crop characteristics, and farmer priorities into an understandable way to explore possible rotation strategies.

This concept responds to the NASA Space Apps challenge: use long-term environmental datasets and science-informed techniques to help farmers protect their fields, conserve water, and strengthen long-term resilience.

The essential questions

  • What is changing?How are temperature, rainfall, soil moisture, and vegetation conditions shifting?
  • What helps?Which rotation choices are associated with healthier soil and vegetation under comparable conditions?
  • What comes next?Which options balance water use, growing seasons, disease risk, markets, and farmer priorities?

The analysis engines

Two ways to understand the future

WHY

Causal analysis

Did cover crops improve the outcome?

Causal analysis estimates what probably changed because of a rotation choice, rather than simply showing that two fields were different.

  1. Define the treatment: cover crop used or not used.
  2. Match comparable fields using rainfall, soil, temperature, terrain, and management.
  3. Compare the observed outcome and report the estimated effect with uncertainty.

Example question: For fields with similar drought exposure, did adding a cover crop increase end-of-season soil moisture?

Illustrative result+0.03 soil moisture
NEXT

Predictive analysis

What conditions should we expect next season?

Predictive analysis learns patterns from past observations to estimate future conditions. It forecasts risk; it does not prove that a strategy caused an outcome.

  1. Build a time series from rainfall, temperature, soil moisture, and vegetation.
  2. Use recent conditions and seasonal patterns as model features.
  3. Generate a forecast, confidence interval, and accuracy score on held-out seasons.

Example question: Given the next season's expected rainfall and current moisture, how likely is elevated vegetation stress?

From practice to outcome

How each rotation method works

Rotation changes what is planted, when the soil is covered, and how much water and nutrients the field uses over multiple seasons.

Strategy A

Conventional rotation

The farmer follows the established cash-crop sequence and manages the field mainly around planting and harvest.

  1. Plant the planned cash crop.
  2. Harvest and prepare the field for the next crop.
  3. Repeat the sequence without an interim cover crop.
Helps withFamiliar equipment and timing
Watch forBare soil, runoff, and nutrient loss
Strategy B

Rotation + cover crops

A cover crop is planted between cash crops so living roots and residue protect the soil during otherwise uncovered periods.

  1. Harvest the primary crop and plant a cover crop.
  2. Keep the soil covered through the vulnerable period.
  3. Terminate the cover crop before it competes with the next cash crop.
Helps withInfiltration, soil structure, and erosion control
Watch forEstablishment cost and water use
Strategy C

Alternative cover crop mix

A locally adapted mix combines species with different rooting depths, water needs, and nutrient roles to balance protection with risk.

  1. Select species for the local climate, soil, and planting window.
  2. Use complementary roots and residue to cover the field.
  3. Terminate the mix using a method compatible with the next crop.
Helps withFlexible soil and nutrient goals
Watch forSpecies competition and management complexity
